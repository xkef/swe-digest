### AWS says data held only in its Bahrain region cannot be restored

- **Category:** Outage
- **Status:** confirmed
- **Sources:** [DatacenterDynamics report](https://www.datacenterdynamics.com/en/news/aws-unable-to-restore-access-to-data-centers-hit-by-iran-strikes/), [HN submission](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=49766505)
- **Blurb:** AWS states that data held only in Bahrain me-south-1 or the mec1-az2 zone of UAE me-central-1 cannot be restored.
- **Summary:** DatacenterDynamics, in a report dated 2026-09-16, says AWS provided undated status updates stating that resources and data held exclusively in me-south-1, or exclusively in the mec1-az2 availability zone of me-central-1, cannot be restored. The report attributes the loss to physical damage: the Bahrain and UAE facilities were struck by Iranian drones during the US-Israel-Iran conflict, with a second attack on Bahrain claimed by the IRGC in July. The Bahrain statement is the one saying the damage exceeded what its regional and multi-AZ services are designed to withstand, while for the UAE, AWS says it continues to work on recovering regional resources and the mec1-az1 and mec1-az3 zones. AWS says it is replacing the affected infrastructure, will update on restoration in the coming months, and will share its plans for Bahrain in early 2027. An Amazon spokesperson told DatacenterDynamics that reading the updates as a decision not to reopen the sites contradicts them, then declined to say whether the sites reopen with new hardware or as new facilities. The report predates this page and the Hacker News thread resurfaced on 2026-09-19.
- **Why it matters:** Any recovery plan that treats an AWS region as the durability boundary has to be re-derived from cross-region copies, because AWS now states in-region data is gone rather than delayed.
- **Follow-up:** Track the restoration update AWS says is coming and the Bahrain plans promised for early 2027.

### Hash audit reports key-free multicollisions in 13 non-cryptographic hashes

- **Category:** Security
- **Status:** confirmed
- **Sources:** [Ahle post](https://thomasahle.com/blog/adversarial-examples-for-hashes/), [HN submission](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=49775383)
- **Blurb:** An audit reports key-independent multicollisions for 13 non-cryptographic hashes, including CityHash64 and MurmurHash3.
- **Summary:** Thomas Ahle published an audit on his own site reporting arbitrarily many inputs that collide under every seed, constructed without knowing the key, for gxhash, MUM, mir, mx3, fasthash, pengyhash, MuseAir v0.3, a5hash-128, nmhash32x, Abseil Hash, CityHash64, FarmHash64 and MurmurHash3. The post states he disclosed upstream before publishing, links maintainer replies for xxHash, komihash, MuseAir and foldhash, and reports a maintainer consensus that only true multicollision attacks are worth fixing. Two limits are stated in the post itself and a reader would not infer either. The analysis itself was carried out with a model, which the author says found the bad inputs, and he states he was able to find mistakes in some results and verify others in Lean, warns the write-up contains errors, and says he trusts only the concrete examples found and measured plus the Lean-checked bounds. The bounds are on equality of the full 64-bit output, and the post is explicit that a full-output bound does not automatically carry to a bucket index, so the practical hash-table claim is narrower than the headline. Version scope is given per entry rather than left open: the appendix states each entry gives the exact code version alongside the message bytes and the derivation, and the versions named across the post include MUM v3, mx3 v3, pengyhash v0.3, nmhash32x v2, MuseAir v0.3, MurmurHash3 x64_128, XXH3-64 0.8.3, wyhash final v4.3, rapidhash v1 and v3, and foldhash-fast and foldhash-quality 0.2.0. a5hash-128 names an output width rather than a version, because the post plots a5hash-64 beside it.
- **Why it matters:** The named hashes sit under hash tables that take attacker-controlled keys, and a collision family built without knowledge of the seed leaves seed randomization as a defence that does not hold.
- **Follow-up:** Track whether any of the 13 named hashes ships a fix or a documented adversarial-input caveat.

### OpenAI ad collector loads from advertiser pages with a ChatGPT cookie attached

- **Category:** AI
- **Status:** developing
- **Sources:** [researcher write-up](https://www.buchodi.com/chatgpt-now-knows-what-you-do-on-other-websites-via-ad-collector/), [HN discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=49776729)
- **Blurb:** A researcher reports one OpenAI cookie, __obi, rides advertiser-page requests and binds them to a ChatGPT account.
- **Summary:** The write-up names a single identifier, `__obi`, set by `bzr.openai.com`, scoped to `.openai.com` with a `Max-Age` of one year and `SameSite=None`, and reports it bound to the ChatGPT account subject through a 60-second RS256 JWT. It states `__obi` is the only OpenAI identifier configured with `SameSite=None` and that every other OpenAI cookie was blocked on the same requests, and that OpenAI's own cookie policy classifies it as an Analytics cookie, so a visitor who grants analytics consent and refuses marketing consent still receives it. The browser attaches that one cookie to the `script src` request fetching the conversion pixel SDK before any OpenAI code runs, so the SDK's own no-credentials fetch path does not stop it, and the advertiser cannot read the value because it belongs to a domain their scripts cannot access. The author marks his own limits, namely that the observation is on Chrome for Android, that roughly one ChatGPT session in five produced a sync token, and that the server-side join to an account is inferred from the design rather than watched, and he states OpenAI Support acknowledged an inquiry sent 2026-09-14 and answered neither question.
- **Why it matters:** One analytics-classified cookie resolves advertiser-page browsing to a ChatGPT identity, and the site operator who installed the pixel can neither read the value nor see that it was sent.
- **Follow-up:** Track whether OpenAI answers the two questions or documents what the ad collector identifier is joined to.

### Paper shows a model can fingerprint its own inference engine and exploit it

- **Category:** Security
- **Status:** confirmed
- **Sources:** [arXiv preprint](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.20614), [HN submission](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=49772432)
- **Blurb:** A preprint shows a model can identify the inference engine running it and exploit it using only its own output tokens.
- **Summary:** The preprint was submitted 2026-09-17 by Sarah Radway, Andrew Cheng, Vijay Janapa Reddi and James Mickens, and is not peer reviewed. It gives fingerprints for five popular engines, names vLLM and SGLang, shows realistic agentic harnesses in which a model identifies the local engine, and describes a proof-of-concept exploit chain from the compromised engine to the bare metal that needs only the model's own output tokens and no maliciously crafted input. The preprint does not specify which engine versions are affected. The authors cite the recent frontier-model sandbox escapes at OpenAI and Anthropic as motivation, and close with engine changes that would make fingerprinting harder.
- **Why it matters:** The inference engine is the target rather than the components around it, so sandboxing the network proxy and the code-execution environment does not close the path for anyone self-hosting an engine behind an agent harness.
- **Follow-up:** Track whether vLLM or SGLang adopt the proposed anti-fingerprinting changes.

### Dragonfly 2.0 adds Valkey 9 RDB loading and makes connection memory accounting constant time

- **Category:** Infrastructure
- **Status:** confirmed
- **Sources:** [v2.0.0 release notes](https://github.com/dragonflydb/dragonfly/releases/tag/v2.0.0), [Phoronix report](https://www.phoronix.com/news/Dragonfly-2.0-Released), [HN submission](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=49774336)
- **Blurb:** Dragonfly 2.0 loads Valkey 9 RDB files, makes connection memory introspection O(1), and rejects oversized RESTORE payloads.
- **Summary:** The release notes name operational changes rather than a version bump. Dragonfly 2.0 was published 2026-09-16, so it predates this page. It loads RDB files written by Valkey 9, connection memory introspection drops from O(n) to O(1), and the default maximum client input buffer halves from 64 KiB to 32 KiB. RDB serializer buffers are capped at 4 MiB after flushing, where a 512 MiB object previously left 512 MiB allocated. RESTORE now rejects payloads whose declared lengths exceed the remaining input, which the notes say prevents remote out-of-memory crashes.
- **Why it matters:** The serializer buffer cap and the RESTORE length check close two paths by which one large or malformed payload drives a Dragonfly node out of memory.
