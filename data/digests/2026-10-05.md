### Denmark says unauthorized parties abused a company's lawful CPR access to reach records on 8.8 million people

- **Category:** Security
- **Status:** confirmed
- **Sources:** [primary](https://cphpost.dk/2026-10-05/life-in-denmark/cpr-data-breach-exposes-personal-details-of-8-8-million-people-in-denmark/), [register](https://cpr.dk/), [discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=49961640)
- **Blurb:** Denmark says unauthorized parties used a company's lawful CPR access to reach records on 8.8 million people.
- **Summary:** The Copenhagen Post report, bylined Ritzau and dated 2026-10-05, states that unauthorized parties used a company's lawful query access to the Danish CPR register to reach records on 8.8 million people, and it carries on-the-record statements from the Ministry of Research, Education and Digitalisation, the CPR administration, Datatilsynet, and Minister Christina Egelund, which is what this block rests `confirmed` on from a single wire report. The report places the incident in September, says the CPR administration became aware of it on the evening of Friday 2026-10-02, and says the review so far found that the names and addresses of people registered for name and address protection were not exposed. The register itself was not compromised, and cpr.dk states that public authorities may retrieve CPR data under section 32 of the CPR law while private access rests on separate provisions of the same law, against a register cpr.dk puts at about 11.4 million persons including nearly 6.1 million living.
- **Why it matters:** The attack path was a partner's legitimate query access rather than a compromise of the register, and roughly a month passed between the September activity and the administration learning of it.
- **Follow-up:** Watch for a primary statement from the ministry or Datatilsynet naming the company and the fields reached beyond names, addresses, and CPR numbers.

### A Go stop-the-world pause stretched to 40ms because the collector's metadata had been swapped out

- **Category:** Languages
- **Status:** confirmed
- **Sources:** [primary](https://frn.sh/go-gc/), [reproduction](https://github.com/frnsimoes/go-gc-swap-cost), [discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=49959654)
- **Blurb:** A Go stop-the-world pause reached 40ms because the kernel had swapped out the collector's own metadata.
- **Summary:** The post, dated 2026-09-13, traces a 40ms stop-the-world pause to garbage collector metadata the kernel had paged out to swap, so the collector read its own bookkeeping from disk with every P halted. The bookkeeping lives outside the heap and is reused rather than freed, which is what lets it go cold enough for the kernel to age it out while the process stays busy. A 2026-09-14 update measures Go 1.26 Green Tea against the same case and reports a negligible change, and the author publishes a reproduction repository alongside the post.
- **Why it matters:** Go GC bookkeeping sits outside the heap and is reused rather than freed, so a host under memory pressure can turn a routine pause into disk latency paid with every P halted.

## Security

### Xray-core certificate pinning skipped hostname verification on two dial paths

- **Category:** Security
- **Status:** confirmed
- **Sources:** [primary](https://github.com/XTLS/Xray-core/security/advisories/GHSA-5wf9-h793-w73c), [discussion](https://github.com/net4people/bbs/issues/672), [further discussion](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=49956003)
- **Blurb:** Xray-core's `pinnedPeerCertSha256` replaced hostname verification, leaving MITM open on the Hysteria2 and gRPC dial paths.
- **Summary:** GHSA-5wf9-h793-w73c, published 2026-07-10, records that setting `pinnedPeerCertSha256` in Xray-core replaced certificate verification rather than adding to it, so hostname checking was skipped whenever `ServerName` ended up empty. The advisory states that Xray-core usually derives `ServerName` from the server address, and scopes the machine-in-the-middle condition to the two paths where it does not: the Hysteria2 dialer, and gRPC dial when the address is an IP address rather than a domain. It lists affected versions from v26.1.13 and patched versions from v26.7.11, rates the severity Low, and records no known CVE, and the net4people thread holds that the first code change addressing the flaw shipped without a security note, which was not verified against the commit and release history here.
- **Why it matters:** Pinning through `pinnedPeerCertSha256` replaces hostname verification instead of adding to it, so on those two dial paths it is weaker than ordinary certificate verification.
